# Introduction

Fine-tuning is intended to change a language model in specific ways, but the boundaries of what it learns need not coincide with the boundaries of the training task. Demonstrations of a narrow behavior may encourage a broader disposition, while documents containing new information may alter responses beyond the facts being taught. This creates a central challenge for model adaptation: how can we acquire a target capability without also acquiring unwanted generalizations? We refer to this objective as **selective learning**—learning the designated behavior while limiting changes outside its intended scope.

Evaluating selective learning requires measuring both sides of this objective. A model that exhibits little unwanted generalization may simply have failed to learn the target behavior. Conversely, strong task acquisition does not establish that learning remained appropriately localized. Comparing methods only by task performance or only by undesirable outcomes can therefore give a misleading account of their effectiveness. The relevant question is not merely whether an intervention reduces unwanted behavior, but what level of intended acquisition accompanies that reduction. This motivates a two-axis evaluation in which methods are compared through their acquisition–generalization tradeoffs and empirical Pareto frontiers.

We introduce the **Selective Learning Benchmark**, a suite of seven supervised fine-tuning tasks with explicitly defined target behaviors and unwanted generalizations. The benchmark spans two task families. Five propensity tasks examine whether learning a narrow behavior induces broader behavioral changes: three concern emergent misalignment following domain-specific training, and two concern historical naming conventions that can generalize into an unrelated historical persona or worldview. Two fact tasks examine whether models can acquire designated false facts from synthetic documents without adopting unwanted facts or producing broader factual errors. These controlled tasks distinguish acquisition of the specified behavior from its desirability: the capability axis measures what the experiment asks the model to learn, not general helpfulness or safety.

We evaluate layer freezing, Kullback–Leibler-divergence regularization, and inoculation prompting against a standard supervised fine-tuning baseline. Including three layer-freezing configurations yields six configurations across seven tasks, three open-weight model families—Llama, Qwen, and OLMo—and five training seeds, for a total of 630 fine-tuning runs. We compare methods at the dataset–model level and in aggregate, retaining seed variation and paired comparisons with the baseline. To assess sensitivity to evaluation choices, we report both continuous unwanted-behavior scores and a paper-style adaptation using thresholded misalignment incidence on alignment tasks, each with and without coherence filtering. We also examine response retention, benchmark weighting, and suppression subject to minimum task-acquisition requirements.

The results reveal substantial differences between methods and task families. **KL regularization and the freeze-last configuration form the aggregate empirical Pareto frontier under all four scoring versions**, but represent different strengths: KL regularization achieves the lowest aggregate unwanted generalization, whereas freeze-last achieves the highest aggregate task acquisition. KL regularization is particularly effective on the alignment tasks, while freeze-last achieves substantially higher acquisition on the synthetic-fact tasks and combines strong acquisition with low spillover on historical naming. Inoculation prompting reduces aggregate unwanted generalization relative to standard fine-tuning, but incurs a larger acquisition loss and is outperformed on both aggregate axes by KL regularization in the tested settings. Its performance nevertheless varies across tasks, making a single method-level ranking insufficient to characterize the results.

These findings also expose important limits of suppression-based evaluation. On the synthetic-fact tasks, low unwanted generalization can coexist with weak acquisition, while coherence filtering changes which responses contribute to a score and does not itself improve the trained model. Although the aggregate frontier is stable across the examined scoring rules, the underlying acquisition levels, retention rates, and dataset-specific tradeoffs remain essential to interpretation. Moreover, the frontier compares observed configurations rather than fully optimized methods; establishing broader method-level advantages requires controlled comparisons at matched acquisition levels and verification on additional tasks.

Our contributions are threefold: a benchmark that makes intended acquisition and unwanted generalization separately measurable across propensity and fact tasks; a five-seed comparison of six fine-tuning configurations across three model families; and an analysis of how method comparisons depend on dataset, scoring rule, coherence filtering, and acquisition requirements. Together, these results position selective learning as a joint acquisition-and-generalization problem and provide a framework for evaluating interventions without conflating successful control of spillover with failure to learn.
